EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

A structurally undersupplied office market meeting an underbuilt flex sector

The UAE runs the tightest prime office market of any major global hub, while its flexible office stock sits at roughly half of London's penetration - on the same demand fundamentals that carried London from 6% to 12% in six years. The spread a flex operator captures over underlying rent in Dubai and Abu Dhabi is wider than in any mature benchmark market - and Abu Dhabi's premium tier has already proven it at 100% occupancy.

SECTION 1

Current state: market share of flex vs traditional

No brokerage publishes a flex census for the UAE, so we triangulate from three independent methods. All three converge on the same conclusion: the UAE already has a meaningful serviced-office base, but modern institutional flex remains a fraction of where comparable demand fundamentals took London.

DIFC, Downtown and One Central sit at effectively zero vacancy (JLL, May 2026). Only ~0.9M sq ft completed in Dubai in 2025 against a 101M sq ft base; meaningful new supply arrives 2027-2028, and roughly a third of it is DIFC stock that is substantially pre-leased (Cushman & Wakefield Core, 2025).

Where flex stands today

Methodology. Three triangulation methods: (a) reported Dubai flex additions of 1M+ sq ft in 2024 at ~15% market growth imply a Dubai flex base of ~6.5-7.5M sq ft including legacy serviced offices; (b) Mordor's $1.12B 2025 UAE flex revenue divided by the ~$580/desk/month UAE average implies ~7-8M sq ft at typical densities and occupancy; (c) flex space reaches up to 8% of leasable area in new Dubai Grade A projects, with flex occupancy above 90%. Result: 4-6% of total stock including the deep serviced-office base; modern coworking-format space alone is ~2-3%. Charts use the 5% midpoint, flagged as a Palm & Peak estimate.

SECTION 2

Growth drivers: where corporate tenants tip from traditional to flex

Six inflection triggers are pushing UAE occupiers toward flexible models. Most are the same forces that drove London's 2015-2019 leg - but three of them are amplified by the UAE's near-zero prime vacancy.

  1. Capital expenditure avoidance. 64% of European occupiers cite avoiding fit-out capex as the top reason for choosing flex (CBRE European Occupier Survey, 2025). Dubai fit-out costs run AED 300-450 per sq ft for a standard corporate build, with reinstatement to shell and core required at exit - a double capex penalty flex removes entirely.
  2. Speed to occupy in a zero-vacancy market. With DIFC, Downtown and One Central effectively full and prime vacancy at 0.1-0.7%, a corporate entrant cannot find fitted Grade A space on conventional timelines. Flex is the only same-month option, which is why enterprise clients already make up 40% of Dubai flexible workspace users (teams of 20+, British Chamber Dubai, 2024).
  3. Lease-term asymmetry. Dubai prime landlords now demand 7-9 year terms, up from 3-5 (JLL, Q3 2025), with ~5% annual escalations - while the average UAE flex commitment is ~14 months. In a market where rents moved +19-32% in a year, term flexibility is a hedge in both directions.
  4. Company-formation velocity. DIFC added 2,525 companies in 2025 (+28% YoY, to 8,844); ADGM licences grew 30% to 12,671 with workforce up 51%. New entrants land with 5-50 people and no local facilities function - the natural flex customer. Abu Dhabi office requirements exceeded 5M sq ft in H1 2025, up 110% YoY (Knight Frank).
  5. Regulatory unlock. ADGM amended its employment regulations in April 2025 to explicitly permit hybrid and remote work, opening flex adoption to regulated financial entities - the deepest-pocketed tenant pool on Al Maryah Island.
  6. Corporate portfolio targets. European occupiers now target 29% of their portfolio in flex by 2027, up from 21% a year earlier (CBRE, 2025); ~70% of CBRE's London flex transactions come from corporates with over 1,000 staff. Enterprise adoption - not startups - is what carried London from 6% to 12%, and the same cohort is arriving in the UAE now.

SECTION 3

Market forecast 2026-2031: three scenarios against the London and NYC cadence

London took roughly 4-5 years to move from ~3.5% to 6.3% penetration (2015-2019) and six more to reach 12% - and CBRE now forecasts 20% by 2030. Manhattan peaked near 3.6%, corrected through the WeWork bankruptcy, and has already regrown to an all-time-high 12.8M sq ft. We model the UAE against both cadences.

What the benchmarks teach. Two lessons transfer directly. First, the enterprise inflection matters more than startup demand: London's move from 6% to 12% was carried by corporates over 1,000 staff. Second, flex demand survives operator failure: Manhattan's inventory hit a record high within 2.5 years of its anchor operator's Chapter 11 - because the correction was a balance-sheet problem (leveraged 15-year leases against 1-year memberships), not a demand problem. The management-agreement model that replaced it - 53-78% of new UK operator deals, 99% of IWG's 2025 signings - is the structure the UAE market will be built on.
SECTION 4

Price analysis: flex vs traditional, and the operator's spread

Two different questions hide inside "is flex more expensive?" For the tenant, mid-market flex in Dubai is cheaper all-in than a traditional lease; premium flex carries only a modest per-person premium. For the operator, density converts those desk rates into 1.9-3.0x the underlying rent per square foot - a wider spread than London or New York, with The Executive Centre's ADGM economics setting the ceiling.

The pricing North Star: The Executive Centre

TEC is the proof that institutional-grade product clears premium pricing in this market. It runs five UAE centres: One Central at DWTC (2018), One Za'abeel (307 desks, managed workspace, 2024), a 53,000 sq ft DMCC entry at Sweid One announced June 2026, and two ADGM centres on Al Maryah Island (Al Sila Tower L12 and Al Maryah Tower L7, 291 workstations). The first ADGM centre reached 100% occupancy within six months of opening, triggering the second and a stated 60,000 sq ft UAE expansion plan (ADGM announcement, Jun 2024).

The rate card: Dubai coworking pass AED 1,100/month, private offices from AED 3,500 per desk; ADGM serviced offices around AED 4,000 per person per month - roughly AED 8,600 per sqm per year of revenue against ADGM headline rents of AED 2,800-3,500 per sqm. This is the tier Palm & Peak underwrites to match or exceed: TEC-level product and pricing, delivered on an AI-native cost base.

The premium tier proves the demand; the spread between tiers is the repricing runway an institutional operator captures.

What "institutional-grade" means in 2026

Six markers, synthesised from CBRE, Savills, Instant Group and JLL 2025-26 research: management-agreement deal structures rather than leveraged leases; a staffed hospitality layer; app-based access, booking and occupancy analytics as standard; branded enterprise suites for 50-250 desk deals; meeting and event revenue diversification; and trophy-building placement.

Measured against that bar, the UAE field divides cleanly. International platforms bring structure and tech but have thin local footprints - Industrious absent, WeWork at a single site. Local operators hold the locations and the relationships but run legacy formats with no revenue management, no churn analytics and no enterprise machinery.

The gap is not locations - it is operating sophistication. Nobody in the market currently combines prime UAE floorspace with the management-agreement economics and data-driven operations that define the asset class's institutional tier. That combination is the whitespace.

SECTION 6

Investment implications: the window this analysis opens

Every element of this report points at the same structural moment. Demand fundamentals (Section 2) are the strongest of any global hub. Penetration (Section 1) sits at half of London's, on the cadence London itself just completed (Section 3). The operator spread (Section 4) is the widest in any benchmarked market - and Abu Dhabi's premium tier has already proven it, with TEC, Regus and Al Maqam clearing 2.2-4x the underlying rent at ADGM Square while the mid-market lags a full tier below. And the supply side (Section 5) has no institutional consolidator: global platforms lack local floorspace, local operators lack the operating layer.

The asset play

Secure Grade A floors in the zero-vacancy core (DIFC, ADGM, prime Dubai free zones) at shell basis and re-let as managed flexible space - capturing the 1.9-3.0x revenue spread documented in Section 4, on management-agreement structures where possible. The pricing North Star is The Executive Centre's ADGM economics: ~AED 4,000 per person at 100% occupancy - the tier Palm & Peak targets to match or exceed.

The operating play

Run the floors on an AI-native operating layer - leasing, billing, facilities, retention - holding headcount flat as the portfolio grows. Palm & Peak already operates its residential business this way in production, not on a roadmap.

The timing

Enter before the 2027-28 supply wave and before Abu Dhabi flex repricing closes the gap. Under the moderate scenario the UAE flex market adds ~$1.4B of annual revenue by 2031; the aggressive case doubles the market's penetration.

The proof point is already on the ground: in Sky Tower on Reem Island, incumbent operators charge AED 2,625-3,500 per desk per month for ADGM-eligible private offices, on legacy product with no institutional operating layer. TEC pricing above, incumbent execution below: that is the gap the platform is built to close.

The author ran enterprise flex economics from inside WeWork through the sector's boom and its correction. The lesson of that cycle is embedded in this thesis: the failure was leverage and lease structure, never demand - and the operators now winning (IWG at 99% capital-light, Industrious inside CBRE) prove the corrected model. The UAE is the one major market where that corrected model can still be built ahead of the penetration curve rather than behind it.
